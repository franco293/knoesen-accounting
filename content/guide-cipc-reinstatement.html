This guide describes CIPC's published reinstatement process. Its completion window and evidence requirements were checked on 15 October 2026 against the 2025 reinstatement practice note and the 2026 evidence notice. CIPC also publishes a step-by-step guide to the automated process. Reinstatement uses CIPC e-Services; check current instructions before applying rather than relying on an older email process.

First, work out which problem you have

"Deregistered" covers two quite different situations, and the route out depends on which one you are in.

Deregistered for non-compliance. You stopped filing annual returns, CIPC referred the company for deregistration, and in due course it was finally deregistered. The company still existed and was still doing something when that happened. This is the ordinary case, and Form CoR40.5 is the answer.

The company was already finished. It had stopped trading, held no assets and had no economic value by the time final deregistration happened. CIPC is explicit about this: re-instatement is not available, and the answer is to register a new company — which costs R175, considerably less than the effort of trying to revive something that had already ended.

The test is not whether the company exists on paper. It is whether it was in business or had other economic value at the time of deregistration. That phrase is the hinge the whole application turns on.

What deregistration actually does

A deregistered company ceases to exist as a legal person. The consequences are not administrative inconveniences:

  • It cannot contract, sue, or be sued in its own name.
  • Its bank accounts can be frozen, and banks generally act on the CIPC register without being asked.
  • Its assets fall to the state as bona vacantia — ownerless goods — until it is reinstated. Property registered in the company's name is the painful version of this.
  • It cannot obtain a tax clearance certificate or a disclosure certificate, which is usually how people find out.

Reinstatement is retrospective: once it goes through, the company is treated as having continued to exist throughout. That is precisely why it matters for anything that happened while it was deregistered.

The evidence rule that catches people out

The 2025 reinstatement practice note replaced Practice Note 1 of 2022. It keeps the requirement that the entity was in business or had economic value at final deregistration, and that supporting evidence is retained. Do not assume an automated application removes the underlying evidence requirement.

The company must still have been in business or had economic value at final deregistration. What changed is only that you no longer attach the proof up front. CIPC states that the evidence must be retained, and that it reserves the right to request it at any time under Companies Regulation 168.

And if you cannot produce it when asked: CIPC may withdraw the re-instatement and the annual return filings that followed it. Keep independently verifiable documents in the entity's name that relate to the period at or around final deregistration, following the 2026 evidence notice. Examples and supporting conditions vary by document type; a director's personal record is not automatically evidence of the company's economic activity.

Submitting false information to CIPC is a criminal offence under section 214 of the Companies Act, and CIPC says so on the first page of its own guide. This is not a form to be optimistic on.

When you need a court order instead

Form CoR40.5 is for the company putting its own house in order. If a creditor or another outside party wants the company reinstated so they can enforce a liability against it, that requires a court order.

CIPC advises that an order should also mandate the company to comply with its obligations within a set period, rather than merely ordering reinstatement. The outstanding requirements include annual returns, the Beneficial Ownership declaration, annual financial statements or a financial accountability supplement, and anything else needed to bring its records up to date.

Reinstatement by court order does not remove those filing duties. The 2025 note says that if the outstanding filings are not made, the entity returns to annual-return deregistration. Obtain legal advice on the appropriate order and who will complete the follow-through filings; the consequence depends on non-compliance, not simply on whether a particular sentence appears in the order.

The process, as it now works

Re-instatement is filed through CIPC e-Services. You need a customer code, which is free to register through BizPortal, and funds loaded against it.

  1. Log in to e-Services with your customer code, then More Services → Company and Close Corporation Reinstatements.
  2. Accept the terms and the mandate. The mandate is now electronic — there is no separate signed document to attach.
  3. Start a new application and select the entity, either from those linked to your code or by searching. If an unfinished application already exists you will be sent to it rather than allowed to start another.
  4. Choose the type — ordinary re-instatement, or court order.
  5. Enter the turnover for each outstanding annual return year. The system prices the whole thing at once: the R200 re-instatement fee plus every outstanding annual return.
  6. Give the industry and the physical address as they were at the time of final deregistration.
  7. Each director or member resolves on the directors' dashboard. Their details cannot be edited here — any correction has to wait until the company is fully reinstated.
  8. Verify by OTP. The code owner captures their ID and an email or SMS OTP. It is valid for 96 hours.
  9. Pay within five working days. Miss that and the application is cancelled and has to be started again from scratch.

For a court order, you upload the order itself — not the court papers — and CIPC's back office confirms its validity.

Paying is not the end of it

This is the step people miss. Processing the CoR40.5 does not put the company back in business. The status only changes once you have also filed:

  • every outstanding annual return;
  • the latest Beneficial Ownership declaration; and
  • the annual financial statements or financial accountability supplement, depending on your public interest score.

Complete the outstanding filings within 30 business days

The 2025 practice note requires all outstanding annual returns, beneficial ownership declarations and AFS/FAS to be filed within 30 business days after the reinstatement application has been processed and paid. Otherwise the entity returns to its previous deregistered status and the reinstatement application process must start again. Payment alone does not complete the process.

Only then does the entity show as in business. And only then can you correct anything else — a director change, an address, the name — through the ordinary amendment forms such as CoR39. Amendments attempted before that point will not take.

What it costs

The re-instatement application itself is R200. The real cost is almost always the outstanding annual returns behind it — one for every year missed, each at the late fee, and for a close corporation with a penalty added on each one.

Registering a fresh company instead costs R175. Where the old company genuinely had no economic value at deregistration, that is not a shortcut — it is the correct answer, and CIPC says so itself. It stops being the right answer the moment the old company held a contract, a lease, a property, a licence or a trading history you need to keep.
