Why this is the employer's problem, not the contractor's

Almost every business we speak to has this the wrong way round. They believe that if the worker invoices them, calls themselves a contractor, and has agreed in writing that they are not an employee, the business is covered.

It is not. Interpretation Note 17 is explicit: it is the responsibility of the employer to work out whether payments are subject to employees' tax — and SARS says the employer holds that duty because the employer "is in the best position to evaluate the facts and the actual situation."

So if SARS later decides the person was really an employee, the PAYE that should have been withheld becomes the employer's liability, together with penalties and interest, for every month it was not deducted. The contractor's invoices, and the contract you both signed, do not move that liability. What matters is the substance of the working relationship, not the label on it.

It is also worth knowing what SARS will not do. Branch offices used to issue "Letters of Independence" confirming a worker's status. That practice has stopped, and the refusal is now written into the Tax Administration Act. There is no letter you can go and fetch. You have to make the determination and be able to defend it.

Step One

The two statutory tests

These come first, and they are conclusive. If a statutory test applies, you have your answer and you never reach the common-law test.

The overriding test: three or more employees

A person who employs three or more full-time employees who are not connected persons in relation to them, and who are engaged in the business throughout the year of assessment, is deemed to be carrying on a trade independently.

This is the overriding test. It beats the first test below even where that test is satisfied, and it beats the common-law position too. If it applies, the person is an independent contractor for PAYE purposes and that is the end of the enquiry.

"Connected person" is doing real work in that sentence. For a natural person it means any relative — a spouse, or anyone related to them or their spouse within the third degree of consanguinity, or the spouse of anyone so related — and any trust of which they or a relative is a beneficiary. A one-person consultancy that "employs" a spouse and two adult children does not pass this test.

The first test: premises plus control

This test deems a person not to be carrying on a trade independently — making their earnings remuneration, and subject to PAYE. It has two parts, and both must apply:

  1. Premises. The services are required to be performed mainly at the premises of the client. "Mainly" is a quantitative measure and means more than 50%. The premises may be those of the person paying the amount or of the person to whom the services are rendered — which are not always the same party.
  2. Control or supervision. The worker is subject to the control or the supervision of any other person as to the manner in which their duties are performed, or as to their hours of work. Either one is enough; you do not need both.

Note how wide the second part is. It says "any other person" — not just you. It can include whoever pays, whoever receives the service, or any other party with a contractual right to control or supervise that work.

One nuance that trips people up: if this test catches someone, they are deemed not to be trading independently and PAYE must be withheld, but that does not make them an employee. Their independent status at common law is unaffected, which is why the limitation on deductions in section 23(m) does not bite them. Where PAYE has been withheld, the amount is reported under code 3616 on the IRP5 rather than 3601.

Step Two

The common-law dominant impression test

Only if neither statutory test settles it do you get here.

There is no single deciding factor. SARS applies a grid of twenty indicators and weighs the overall impression they create, sorted into three bands by how much they count. The question each one is really asking is whether the client has acquired the person's productive capacity — their labour — or has instead bought a result from an independent business.

Near-conclusive — these carry the most weight

  • Control of the manner in which the work is done
  • Payment regime — paid by the hour, week or month, or paid for a deliverable
  • Who must render the service — must it be that person, or may they send someone else
  • Nature of the obligation to work
  • Client base — one client, or several
  • Risk, profit and loss — who carries it

Persuasive — the degree of control over the work environment

  • Instructions or supervision
  • Reports the worker must submit
  • Training provided by the client
  • Productive time — control of working hours and the working week

Resonant — superficial, but still counted

  • Whose tools, materials and stationery are used
  • Whether the worker has their own office or workshop
  • Integration into the client's usual premises
  • Integration into the client's usual business operations

SARS is direct about how this should be used: the grid is a guide and not a scorecard. You cannot tally ticks in each column and declare a winner. Each indicator has to be considered on its own facts, and some become irrelevant while others matter more as the relationship changes over time.

A useful way to sanity-check the result: an independent business is an entrepreneurial enterprise with enough independence that it would survive the termination of the relationship with this client. If losing you would simply end the person's livelihood, you are probably looking at an employee.

Two Special Cases

Labour brokers and personal service providers

Labour brokers

Payments to a labour broker who does not hold a valid exemption certificate (IRP30) are subject to employees' tax. SARS describes this as an anti-avoidance measure that "must be applied strictly" — so if you are paying a labour broker, ask for the IRP30 before you pay, not afterwards. The certificate is issued only where the broker genuinely carries on an independent business, tested against the same common-law indicators above.

Personal service providers

Interposing a company or trust between the worker and the client does not escape any of this. Where the arrangement meets the definition of a personal service provider, PAYE applies to payments made to that entity, and it is also one of the disqualifications from Small Business Corporation rates — which we cover in the small business tax guide.

In Practice

What to actually do

  1. Run the overriding test first. Does this person employ three or more full-time, unconnected employees all year? If yes, you are done — they are independent.
  2. Then the first test. Do they work mainly at your premises and are they subject to control or supervision over how or when they work? If both, withhold PAYE.
  3. Only then, the dominant impression. Weigh the indicators honestly against the facts as they are, not as the contract describes them.
  4. Write down your reasoning and keep it. There is no letter from SARS to rely on. Your file note, made at the time and supported by the facts, is what you will have when the question is asked years later.
  5. Re-check when things change. Status is not decided once. A contractor who starts out with six clients and ends up working only for you, at your desk, on your hours, has quietly become something else.
