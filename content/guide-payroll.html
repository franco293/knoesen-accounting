Step one: register as an employer

Check PAYE, SDL and UIF separately when you hire staff. Registration and payment requirements depend on the employer and employees, not only the number of people on the payroll:

  • PAYE: registration with SARS is generally required within 21 business days after becoming an employer, unless none of the employees are liable for normal tax.
  • SDL: an employer whose anticipated remuneration subject to SDL does not exceed R500 000 over the next 12 months is exempt and does not need to register to pay SDL on that basis. Above this threshold, check whether another exemption applies.
  • UIF: contributions for covered employees are paid through SARS or the UIF Commissioner, as applicable. Coverage exclusions include an employee working for that employer for less than 24 hours a month; check the full rules for your employees.

A PAYE registration exception does not automatically remove UIF obligations. Confirm the appropriate registration, declaration and contribution arrangements rather than assuming every employer must pay the same contribution to both SARS and the UIF office.

The Cycle

What happens, and when

Every month — the EMP201

The EMP201 declares the PAYE, UIF and SDL for the month and is accompanied by payment. It is due within seven days after the end of the month the deductions relate to. Where the seventh falls on a weekend or public holiday, the deadline moves earlier, to the last business day before it.

Late payment attracts a penalty on the amount plus interest at {{interest_tax_rate}}% per annum from {{interest_tax_effective}} (rates and earlier periods). This is one of the deadlines SARS enforces most consistently, because the money was withheld from employees and is not really the employer's to hold.

Twice a year — the EMP501

The employer reconciliation compares three things: what you declared on your monthly EMP201s, what you actually paid over, and what appears on the IRP5 and IT3(a) certificates issued to employees. All three have to agree.

  • Interim reconciliation — covering March to August, submitted in the September–October window.
  • Annual reconciliation — covering the full tax year, submitted in the April–May window. This is the one that produces the IRP5 certificates your employees need to file their own returns.

A reconciliation that does not balance is not a paperwork problem. It flows straight through to your employees' assessments, and they will come back to you.

Once a year — IRP5 certificates

Generated from the annual EMP501, IRP5 certificates are pre-populated into employees' income tax returns. If the certificate is wrong, the employee's return is wrong. This is where payroll errors become visible to your staff.

Getting It Wrong

The five expensive mistakes

1. Calling an employee a contractor

Whether someone is genuinely an independent contractor depends on the substance of the relationship, not the wording of the agreement or the fact that they invoice you. The tests look at control over how and when the work is done, integration into your operations, who carries the risk, and whether the person works for others.

If SARS concludes the substance was employment, it can recover the PAYE that should have been withheld — from the employer, together with penalties and interest, even though the money was already paid to the worker.

There are two conclusive statutory tests before you ever reach that judgement call, and one of them can settle the question outright. We set all of them out in contractor or employee? what SARS actually tests.

2. Leaving fringe benefits off the payroll

Company vehicles, cellphones, subsidised accommodation, low-interest loans and employer medical scheme contributions can create taxable benefits. Apply the category's exclusions, valuation and PAYE rules before calculating the taxable value. For example, employer-provided telephone equipment used mainly for the employer's business can have no taxable value under SARS's rules. Keep records supporting that business use rather than treating every cellphone as a taxable perk.

Leaving a benefit's taxable value out can understate PAYE. Vehicle, accommodation and loan arrangements have their own conditions; the official loan interest rate is effective-dated, so use the rate that applies to the relevant period.

3. Paying the EMP201 late because cash was tight

Understandable, and still one of the worst things to do. PAYE and UIF were withheld from employees; using them as short-term working capital creates a liability that grows with penalties and interest and is very difficult to argue away afterwards.

4. Ignoring the interim EMP501

The August interim reconciliation is the one employers skip. It exists to catch errors halfway through the year, while six months of payroll can still be corrected relatively easily rather than twelve.

5. Not claiming the Employment Tax Incentive

Where you employ qualifying younger workers, the Employment Tax Incentive reduces the PAYE you pay over. It is claimed through the EMP201, and employers who do not know about it simply pay more than they need to. It has specific age, wage and qualifying-employer conditions that are worth checking against your actual payroll.

Beyond Tax

What else an employer owes

Payroll compliance is not only a SARS matter. The Basic Conditions of Employment Act sets requirements for what a payslip must show, when wages must be paid, how leave accrues and what happens on termination. The Compensation for Occupational Injuries and Diseases Act requires registration with the Compensation Fund and an annual return of earnings. Depending on your sector, a bargaining council or sectoral determination may set minimum wages and conditions that override your own arrangements.

These sit outside a tax practice's scope, but they share the same payroll data — which is why it pays to have the payroll itself accurate and current.
