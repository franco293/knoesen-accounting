Company tax, SBC rates and turnover tax

This guide compares standard company tax, Small Business Corporation rates and turnover tax, with a sole proprietor example alongside them. Eligibility matters as much as the rate: not every option is available to every business.

  1. Standard company tax — a flat 27% of taxable income. The default for any company that does not qualify for anything better.
  2. Small Business Corporation (SBC) rates — a graduated scale starting at 0%, available to qualifying companies, close corporations and co-operatives under section 12E of the Income Tax Act.
  3. Turnover tax — a simplified system for micro businesses with turnover of R2.3 million or less, charged on taxable turnover instead of profit. Qualification and registration conditions apply; VAT registration can coexist with it.

A sole proprietor normally includes business profit in their personal income and uses the individual tax tables, up to a top marginal rate of 45%. A qualifying sole proprietor can instead register for turnover tax. See SARS's turnover tax overview.

Worked Example

Company, SBC and sole proprietor: tax on R500 000 profit

An illustrative comparison using R500 000 of taxable income for the year, assuming the requirements for each treatment are met. Turnover tax is compared separately below because it uses turnover, not profit.

Tax payable on R500 000 profit for a standard company, Small Business Corporation and sole proprietor
RegimeCalculationTax
Standard company 27% × R500 000 R135 000
Small Business Corporation R18 620 + 21% × (R500 000 − R365 000) R46 970
Sole proprietor (same profit, no other income) R79 998 + 31% × (R500 000 − R383 100), less the R17 820 primary rebate R98 417

Qualifying as an SBC is worth R88 030 on this profit, against the standard company rate. That is not a rounding difference — it is a salary.

But company tax is not the end of the story

Money in a company is not money in your pocket. Taking it out as a dividend costs another 20% in dividends tax:

  • SBC tax on R500 000 profit: R46 970
  • Leaves R453 030, taxed at 20% on distribution: R90 606
  • Total tax: R137 576 — an effective rate of about 27.5%

The sole proprietor paid R98 417 in this example. This illustrates why comparing company tax alone can be misleading: how you draw or retain profits changes the overall comparison.

This is why "should I register a company to save tax?" rarely has a one-line answer. Limited liability, credibility with customers, and access to finance are often better reasons to incorporate than the tax itself.

SBC

Do you qualify as a Small Business Corporation?

SBC status is not something you elect — you either meet the section 12E requirements or you do not. In broad terms, all of the following must hold for the year of assessment:

  • The entity is a close corporation, co-operative or private company
  • All shareholders or members are natural persons throughout the year — one corporate shareholder disqualifies the entity entirely
  • No shareholder or member holds shares or an interest in any other company or close corporation, apart from certain permitted exceptions such as listed shares and dormant entities
  • Gross income for the year does not exceed the prescribed limit
  • Not more than 20% of total receipts and accruals consists of investment income and income from rendering a personal service
  • The entity is not a "personal service provider" as defined

The shareholding conditions are where most businesses fall over. An owner who holds shares in a second, unrelated company can disqualify an otherwise-qualifying business unless a permitted exception applies. It is worth checking rather than assuming.

Turnover Tax

Turnover tax: rates, examples and trade-offs

Turnover tax uses taxable turnover rather than profit. Businesses with a qualifying annual turnover of R2.3 million or less may elect it — sole proprietors, partnerships, close corporations, companies and co-operatives, subject to the other qualification requirements.

Where turnover tax wins

A qualifying micro business turning over R900 000 with R400 000 of costs and R500 000 of profit pays turnover tax of 1% × (R900 000 − R600 000) = R3 000. The same profit under SBC rates would attract R46 970, assuming SBC qualification. This arithmetic example assumes all turnover is taxable and does not establish eligibility for either regime.

Where it goes badly wrong

A retailer turning over R2 000 000 on thin margins, with R1 900 000 of costs and R100 000 of profit, pays turnover tax of R12 500 + 3% × R600 000 = R30 500 — on a profit of R100 000. Under SBC rates the same business would pay R70.

Turnover tax can remain payable in a loss-making year if taxable turnover exceeds the tax-free band. Costs do not reduce taxable turnover in the way they reduce profit.

Check eligibility and VAT before electing turnover tax

VAT registration is compatible with turnover tax. SARS confirms that a registered micro business can also register for VAT. Consider VAT obligations and input claims separately when comparing the options. See SARS's small-business VAT guidance.

Professional services can disqualify a business. For a natural person, professional-service receipts must not exceed 20% of total receipts; for a company, the limit covers professional services and investment income together. Consulting is included in the professional-service definition. See SARS's turnover tax FAQs.

Getting out is not instant. Deregistering from turnover tax and returning to the normal system is subject to conditions and timing, so it is not a decision to reverse casually each year.

Deciding

Compare the tax options for your business

This is a starting point, not a substitute for running your own numbers:

  • Turnover under R2.3m and high margins → check all turnover tax qualification requirements before comparing the cost.
  • Turnover under R2.3m but thin margins or significant input costs → compare turnover tax with the normal system and test SBC qualification if you operate through a company.
  • A company meeting the section 12E conditions → SBC rates may apply; review qualification when ownership changes.
  • Profits you intend to draw out in full each year → compare the personal tax and any tax on distributions as well as the company's tax.
  • Profits you intend to reinvest → a company retaining earnings needs to be compared with the other available options.
