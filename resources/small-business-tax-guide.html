Small Business

How small businesses are taxed in South Africa

There are three different ways a small South African business can be taxed, and the gap between them is worth real money. Here is how to tell which one applies to you.

Last reviewed 3 August 2026 against SARS and National Treasury sources.

All figures below are for the 2026/27 tax year (1 March 2026 – 28 February 2027), taken from National Treasury's Budget 2026 Tax Guide. Reviewed 2026-08-03.

The three regimes

A small South African business is taxed under one of three systems. The difference between them is not marginal — on the same profit, the tax can vary by a factor of three.

  1. Standard company tax — a flat 27% of taxable income. The default for any company that does not qualify for anything better.
  2. Small Business Corporation (SBC) rates — a graduated scale starting at 0%, available to qualifying companies, close corporations and co-operatives under section 12E of the Income Tax Act.
  3. Turnover tax — a simplified system for micro businesses with turnover of R2.3 million or less, charged on turnover instead of profit and replacing income tax, VAT, provisional tax, capital gains tax and dividends tax.

A sole proprietor is not on any of these: their business profit is simply added to their personal income and taxed on the individual tax tables, up to a top marginal rate of 45%.

Worked Example

The same R500 000 profit, three ways

A company with taxable income of R500 000 for the year. Nothing else changes except which regime applies.

Tax payable on R500 000 profit under each of the three regimes
RegimeCalculationTax
Standard company 27% × R500 000 R135 000
Small Business Corporation R18 620 + 21% × (R500 000 − R365 000) R46 970
Sole proprietor (same profit, no other income) R79 998 + 31% × (R500 000 − R383 100), less the R17 820 primary rebate R98 417

Qualifying as an SBC is worth R88 030 on this profit, against the standard company rate. That is not a rounding difference — it is a salary.

But company tax is not the end of the story

Money in a company is not money in your pocket. Taking it out as a dividend costs another 20% in dividends tax:

  • SBC tax on R500 000 profit: R46 970
  • Leaves R453 030, taxed at 20% on distribution: R90 606
  • Total tax: R137 576 — an effective rate of about 27.5%

The sole proprietor paid R98 417 on the same profit, with the money already in their hands. So the company only wins if profits stay in the business and get reinvested — or if they are extracted as salary rather than dividends, which puts you back on the individual tables anyway.

This is why "should I register a company to save tax?" rarely has a one-line answer. Limited liability, credibility with customers, and access to finance are often better reasons to incorporate than the tax itself.

SBC

Do you qualify as a Small Business Corporation?

SBC status is not something you elect — you either meet the section 12E requirements or you do not. In broad terms, all of the following must hold for the year of assessment:

  • The entity is a close corporation, co-operative or private company
  • All shareholders or members are natural persons throughout the year — one corporate shareholder disqualifies the entity entirely
  • No shareholder or member holds shares or an interest in any other company or close corporation, apart from certain permitted exceptions such as listed shares and dormant entities
  • Gross income for the year does not exceed the prescribed limit
  • Not more than 20% of total receipts and accruals consists of investment income and income from rendering a personal service
  • The entity is not a "personal service provider" as defined

The shareholding conditions are where most businesses fall over. An owner who holds shares in a second, unrelated company — even a dormant one they forgot about — can disqualify an otherwise-qualifying business. It is worth checking rather than assuming.

Turnover Tax

The micro business option — and its trap

Turnover tax is genuinely simple. One calculation on turnover, and it replaces income tax, VAT, provisional tax, capital gains tax and dividends tax. Businesses with a qualifying annual turnover of R2.3 million or less may elect it — sole proprietors, partnerships, close corporations, companies and co-operatives.

Where turnover tax wins

A consultant turning over R900 000 with R400 000 of costs and R500 000 of profit pays turnover tax of 1% × (R900 000 − R600 000) = R3 000. The same profit under SBC rates would attract R46 970. High-margin, low-cost, simple businesses do extremely well here.

Where it goes badly wrong

A retailer turning over R2 000 000 on thin margins, with R1 900 000 of costs and R100 000 of profit, pays turnover tax of R12 500 + 3% × R600 000 = R30 500 — on a profit of R100 000. Under SBC rates the same business would pay R70.

And in a loss-making year, turnover tax is still payable. Turnover tax is charged on what comes in, not on what you keep. That is the whole trade-off: administrative simplicity in exchange for a system that does not care whether you made money.

Two more things before electing turnover tax

You cannot be VAT-registered. Turnover tax replaces VAT, so you lose all input VAT claims. For a business with significant purchases, that alone can outweigh the saving.

Getting out is not instant. Deregistering from turnover tax and returning to the normal system is subject to conditions and timing, so it is not a decision to reverse casually each year.

Deciding

A rough decision path

This is a starting point, not a substitute for running your own numbers:

  • Turnover under R2.3m, high margins, few purchases, no need for VAT registration → turnover tax is likely cheapest.
  • Turnover under R2.3m but thin margins or significant input costs → almost certainly stay on the normal system and test SBC qualification.
  • A company meeting the section 12E conditions → SBC rates, and keep the shareholding clean so you do not lose them.
  • Profits you intend to draw out in full each year → the sole proprietor / salary route is often no worse, and simpler.
  • Profits you intend to reinvest → a company retaining earnings at SBC rates is hard to beat.

The calculation is worth doing properly once, and revisiting whenever turnover or margins shift materially. We are happy to run it with you.
