Getting Started

Starting a business in South Africa: the checklist

Every registration a new South African business needs, in the order you actually need them, and the ongoing obligations each one creates.

Last reviewed 3 August 2026 against SARS and National Treasury sources.

Step 1 — Choose a structure before you register anything

This is the decision everything else hangs off, and it is much cheaper to make deliberately than to unwind later.

South African business structures compared: liability, taxation and what each suits
Structure Liability How profits are taxed Suits
Sole proprietor Unlimited — your personal assets are exposed Added to your personal income, up to 45% Low-risk, early-stage, one person
Partnership Unlimited, and joint — you carry your partners' liabilities too Each partner taxed on their share personally Professionals; needs a written agreement
Private company (Pty) Ltd Limited to what you put in, with exceptions 27%, or SBC rates from 0% if it qualifies Anything with real risk, staff, or growth plans
Trust Assets held separately from you Up to 45%, or taxed in beneficiaries' hands Asset holding and estate planning, not usually trading
Non-profit company (NPC) Limited Possible exemptions if approved as a PBO by SARS Public benefit activities

Close corporations can no longer be newly registered in South Africa, though existing ones may continue to operate.

The most common mistake is choosing a company purely for the tax rate. Limited liability, being able to bring in a shareholder, and looking credible to larger customers are usually better reasons — and, as our small business tax guide shows, once you factor in getting money back out of the company, the tax advantage is smaller than it first appears.

The Sequence

Registrations, in the order you need them

01

Reserve a name and register at CIPC

Register the company with the Companies and Intellectual Property Commission. You can reserve a name first, or register with the registration number as the name and add a trading name later. You will receive a registration certificate and a Memorandum of Incorporation — keep both; every other institution will ask for them.

02

Register for income tax at SARS

A newly registered company is generally registered for income tax automatically through CIPC, but you should confirm the income tax number exists and is active rather than assume it. Sole proprietors use their personal income tax number — the business is not a separate taxpayer.

03

Set up eFiling and appoint a public officer

Every company must appoint a public officer, who is the individual SARS holds responsible for the company's tax affairs. Without one, the company cannot properly transact with SARS. Set up the eFiling profile at the same time.

04

Open a business bank account

Do this before you trade, not after. Mixing business and personal money is the single most common cause of unusable accounting records and, for a company, it undermines the separation that limited liability depends on. Banks will want the CIPC documents, the MOI, and identification for directors.

05

Register as an employer — if you have staff

PAYE and SDL with SARS, and UIF with both SARS and the Department of Employment and Labour. This applies from your first employee, and includes you if the company pays you a salary. See the payroll compliance guide.

06

Consider VAT — but only if it helps

Registration is compulsory once taxable supplies exceed R2.3 million in twelve months. Voluntary registration is possible above R120 000. Voluntary registration is worth it mainly if you sell to VAT-registered businesses or carry significant input VAT — our VAT guide works through the decision.

07

Register with the Compensation Fund

Employers must register under the Compensation for Occupational Injuries and Diseases Act and submit an annual return of earnings. This one is routinely forgotten until a letter of good standing is needed for a contract or tender.

08

Set up bookkeeping from day one

Not month nine, when the first return is due. Records started at the beginning are cheap to maintain; records reconstructed backwards from a bank statement and a shoebox are expensive, and always less accurate.

What Follows

The obligations you have just created

Each registration above starts a recurring cycle. This is what your first full year looks like.

Recurring obligations created by each business registration
Because you registered for…You now owe, recurring
CIPC Annual return plus beneficial ownership declaration, within 30 business days after the anniversary of incorporation
Income tax (company) ITR14 annual return, plus two provisional tax estimates each year
PAYE / UIF / SDL EMP201 monthly within seven days of month end; EMP501 reconciliation twice a year; IRP5 certificates annually
VAT VAT201 return and payment each tax period
Compensation Fund Annual return of earnings
Being a company at all Annual financial statements, and accounting records retained for seven years

A short list of avoidable early mistakes

  • Trading through a personal bank account. Fixable, but every month it continues makes the eventual clean-up more expensive.
  • Registering for VAT because it "looks professional". It creates a permanent return cycle and, if you sell to consumers, makes you 15% more expensive for no benefit.
  • Forgetting the CIPC annual return in year two. Year one is remembered. Year two is where deregistration processes start.
  • Paying yourself with no structure. Salary, dividend and loan account draws have completely different tax consequences, and the difference is decided by how you record it, not by intention.
  • No shareholders' agreement with a co-founder. Not a tax issue, but the one that most often ends otherwise viable businesses.
