VAT

The VAT registration threshold is now R2.3 million

On 1 April 2026 the compulsory VAT registration threshold more than doubled. If your turnover sits between R1 million and R2.3 million, this directly affects you.

Last reviewed 3 August 2026 against SARS and National Treasury sources.

Compulsory registration R2.3m was R1 million until 1 April 2026
Voluntary registration R120 000 taxable supplies per annum
Standard VAT rate 15% unchanged in Budget 2026

Per National Treasury's Budget 2026 Tax Guide and SARS. Reviewed 2026-08-03.

What actually changed

Until 31 March 2026, any business making taxable supplies of more than R1 million in any twelve-month period was obliged to register for VAT. From 1 April 2026, that threshold became R2.3 million.

The voluntary registration threshold moved too: a business making taxable supplies of more than R120 000 but not more than R2.3 million per annum may apply to register voluntarily.

The standard rate itself did not change. VAT is levied at 15%. The increases to 15.5% and then 16% that were proposed in 2025 were reversed, and Budget 2026 left the rate alone.

Why you will still see "R1 million" everywhere

A great deal of South African tax content online has not been updated since the change. If a page tells you the VAT threshold is R1 million and does not show a review date, it is out of date — check the figure against SARS or the current Budget Tax Guide before acting on it.

Who this affects

If your turnover is under R2.3 million and you are not registered — nothing has changed for you, except that you now have considerably more room to grow before registration becomes compulsory.

If your turnover is between R1 million and R2.3 million and you are registered — you are now registered by choice rather than by obligation. That is worth a deliberate decision rather than inertia.

If you were about to cross R1 million — the pressure is off. Businesses that were structuring around the old threshold, or holding back on work to stay under it, no longer need to.

Should you deregister?

This is the real question, and the answer turns almost entirely on who your customers are.

Staying registered usually makes sense if you sell to businesses

A VAT-registered customer claims back the VAT you charge them, so your 15% costs them nothing — while you continue claiming input VAT on your own purchases, rent, equipment and stock. Deregistering in this position means losing your input VAT claims for no benefit to anyone.

Deregistering may make sense if you sell to the public

A consumer cannot claim the VAT back. If you can drop your prices by up to 15% and remain profitable, you become genuinely cheaper than VAT-registered competitors — or you keep prices where they are and the VAT becomes margin. Hairdressers, small retailers, guesthouses, restaurants and trades working for homeowners are the classic cases.

Costs of deregistering that people forget

  • Exit VAT. On deregistration you are generally deemed to have supplied the assets still held in the business, and output VAT becomes payable on them. A business holding vehicles, plant or significant stock can face a real bill on the way out.
  • Lost input VAT going forward. Every future purchase becomes 15% more expensive in real terms, including fuel, equipment, rent on commercial premises and professional fees.
  • Perception. Some larger customers and tenders treat a VAT number as a proxy for scale and legitimacy. It should not matter, but sometimes it does.
  • Re-registering later. If you cross R2.3 million after deregistering, you must register again — with the administrative effort that involves.

How to decide

It is an arithmetic question, not a philosophical one. Work out, for a normal year:

  1. What proportion of your sales are to VAT-registered customers
  2. Your total input VAT claimed in a year
  3. The exit VAT you would pay on assets held today
  4. Whether you could actually hold your prices if you deregistered

If most of your customers are consumers, your input VAT is small, and you hold few assets, deregistration is often clearly better. If you sell business-to-business or you are capital-intensive, staying registered usually wins. In between, it needs modelling — we can run the numbers with you.

When registration is still compulsory

You must register if your taxable supplies exceeded R2.3 million in the preceding twelve months, or where there is a written contractual obligation that will take you over that figure in the coming twelve months. "Taxable supplies" means standard-rated and zero-rated supplies — not exempt supplies, and not turnover you have simply forecast optimistically.

Thresholds and rate per National Treasury's Budget 2026 Tax Guide (1 March 2026 – 28 February 2027) and SARS. Reviewed 2026-08-03.
